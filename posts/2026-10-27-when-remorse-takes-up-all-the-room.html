When Remorse Takes Up All the Room

In Justine’s prison room, Victor’s anguish raises an uncomfortable question about whose suffering receives our attention.

Justine is in prison, facing execution for a crime Victor believes she did not commit. Elizabeth stays close to her, offering affection and affirming her innocence. Victor retreats to a corner, overwhelmed by what he understands as his own responsibility. Yet from that corner he makes a startling comparison: he presents his anguish as deeper than the anguish of the woman who will die the next morning.

His suffering is plainly intense. He cannot answer when Justine asks whether he believes her guilty, and Elizabeth answers for him. But his claim to suffer more reaches beyond anything he can know. He can describe his own torment; he cannot measure Justine’s against it. One way to read this moment is that remorse has made him intensely attentive to his inner life while narrowing his attention to hers.

The movement of the scene makes that possibility especially uncomfortable. Hearing Victor groan, Justine approaches him. Soon she is expressing gratitude for his belief in her innocence. The person awaiting death finds herself responding to the distress of someone who will leave the prison alive. Nothing here establishes that Victor deliberately seeks comfort. The troubling effect does not require a deliberate plan: his visible anguish draws care toward him.

An ordinary mistake can produce a smaller version of this shift. Suppose someone forgets to submit a shared application, costing a friend an opportunity. When they explain what happened, they become so distressed about being unreliable that the friend starts reassuring them. The lost opportunity remains, but the conversation now revolves around whether the person who forgot is still a decent friend. An admission of fault has become another demand on the person affected.

That does not make every tear manipulative or every expression of guilt selfish. Keeping emotion perfectly controlled can be impossible, and a flat apology can feel evasive. The useful distinction may concern what the conversation leaves room for. Can the friend express anger without having to soften it? Can they describe the inconvenience without triggering another round of self-condemnation? Remorse can be sincere and still make those responses harder.

Victor’s narration shows how easily intensity can become a kind of evidence. He repeatedly describes the extremity of his torment, and those descriptions can invite a reader to recognize how much he cares. Yet the strength of a feeling does not tell us what it accomplishes for another person. Justine receives some comfort from knowing that her visitors believe her. Victor’s account also records that their appeals fail and that his intended avowal remains unspoken. His pain cannot settle the question of what he owes her.

In the hypothetical missed application, a more spacious response might include a brief acknowledgment of guilt, followed by attention to practical consequences. Perhaps there is an appeal to make, a fee to repay, or simply anger to hear without interruption. None of that guarantees forgiveness. It does allow the affected friend to remain a person with needs and judgments, rather than becoming the audience for a performance of remorse.

Justine’s tenderness makes the prison scene harder to resolve. Her kindness matters to her, and it would be another intrusion to declare that she should withhold it. Still, a reader can notice how much care she gives while receiving so little protection. Victor’s anguish occupies many sentences; her remaining life occupies very little time. The imbalance leaves an uneasy possibility: feeling consumed by responsibility can coexist with taking up more than one’s share of the room.

A question to sit with

When guilt enters a difficult conversation, what would help you notice whether the other person still has room to speak?
