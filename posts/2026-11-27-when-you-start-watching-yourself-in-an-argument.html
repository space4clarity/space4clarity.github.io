When You Start Watching Yourself in an Argument

Thoreau’s inner spectator helps explain why taking a step back during an argument can change what the other person feels able to say.

In Walden, Thoreau recalls how much he enjoys being shut indoors by a long rainstorm. The steady pelting soothes him, and the early twilight gives his thoughts time to unfold. People who know where he lives imagine that rainy days must make him especially lonely. As he considers their question, he describes a curious kind of company available even in an empty house. Part of him can have an experience while another part stands back and watches it happen.

He compares these two positions to being a piece of driftwood swept along by a stream and looking down on the stream from the sky. The distance can be considerable. A play may move him when an actual event that seems to concern him more leaves him relatively untouched. Yet just as he has made this freedom sound appealing, he adds a short sentence about how it may make us poor neighbors and friends. He leaves the reader to imagine what that might look like.

One possibility is that the spectator can leave a scene earlier than the person beside us. Suppose two friends are washing dishes after dinner when one says she was embarrassed by a joke the other made about her in front of the group. The friend holding the dish towel feels an immediate urge to explain that everybody had been teasing everybody else. Then she catches herself. She can hear how defensive that would sound, and the pause gives her friend time to say what embarrassed her.

The same pause could go another way. The friend with the towel might get so interested in watching her own reaction that she begins to think of the conversation as a small test of how well she handles criticism. She keeps her voice level, waits her turn, and offers an apology. By the time she hangs up the towel, she may feel relieved at how smoothly the exchange went. Her friend may still be standing at the sink trying to work out whether she can say that the joke bothered her because it was the third one like it that evening.

From the outside, those two pauses might look identical. The difference would show up in what the friend with the towel is ready to hear next. If she takes her own composure as a sign that the conversation has gone well, the mention of the other jokes may feel like a fresh accusation. From her friend’s side, it may be the first time she has managed to say what she came into the kitchen to say. Each woman has a different sense of how far along they are.

Thoreau’s word sometimes leaves room for both outcomes. A little distance can help someone stay in a conversation that would otherwise turn into a quick exchange of defenses. It can also let her feel finished while someone else is still weighing what to say. At the sink, there may be no obvious sign of which kind of quiet has settled between the two friends. It could take the next awkward sentence, if there is time enough to risk it, for either of them to find out.

A question to sit with

When you feel calmer halfway through a difficult conversation, what helps you tell whether the other person has had room to say what they came to say?
