What a Creator Owes Before Turning Away

On a mountain glacier, Frankenstein faces a troubling obligation: to hear the being he created without first deciding that listening means forgiveness.

Frankenstein meets the being he created on a glacier, where the creature moves easily across ice that Frankenstein has crossed with caution. Their physical difference is immediately clear. So is their hostility. Frankenstein wants to destroy him; the creature demands that his maker hear him. Before either can offer an account of what has happened, the encounter nearly becomes a fight.

The creature’s appeal is difficult to receive cleanly. He describes anguish, exclusion, and a longing for affection, but he also threatens to kill Frankenstein’s remaining friends. His suffering does not make those threats harmless. The passage holds a plea for care beside an attempt at coercion, leaving Frankenstein with something harder than an innocent victim whose needs would be easy to acknowledge.

Yet the creature asks for one thing that can be considered separately from his conditions: a hearing. He wants to tell his story before Frankenstein decides what he deserves. His appeal to Adam gives that request a particular weight. He understands himself as a being who should have had a place in his creator’s care. Instead, he experiences his maker as another force driving him away. That is his account, which the scene has not yet allowed him to explain.

Frankenstein initially treats the creature’s presence as unbearable in itself. He wants him gone from sight, and says his own misery has left him unable to consider whether he is acting justly. One way to read this is that the creature confronts him with a responsibility he would rather experience only as regret. Cursing the act of creation lets him condemn his past action. Listening would require attention to someone who still exists because of it.

The shift comes when Frankenstein agrees to follow him to shelter. His motives remain mixed: curiosity, compassion, and a desire to discover whether the creature murdered his brother. He has not suddenly become affectionate or trusting. Nevertheless, he recognizes duties that he had not previously felt. In this scene, responsibility begins with allowing the creature’s experience to interrupt the account Frankenstein already has.

An ordinary version of that difficulty might arise when someone starts a neighborhood group. At first, creating it means choosing a name, inviting people, and arranging a first meeting. Months later, a member explains that the meeting times exclude shift workers, or that a few established friends dominate every discussion. The organizer may feel accused after doing considerable unpaid work. It would be tempting to answer by listing good intentions, or to dismiss the complaint because the person delivering it has been rude.

A neighborhood group is not a living creature, and an organizer cannot guarantee everyone’s happiness. The useful connection is narrower: bringing something into existence can create reasons to hear how others experience it. The complaint might be unfair in places. The member might also owe others an apology. Neither possibility settles whether the meeting arrangements exclude people. Listening gives the organizer a chance to distinguish those questions instead of letting irritation answer all of them.

Frankenstein’s decision offers no comfortable resolution. The creature enters the hut exultant; Frankenstein enters with a heavy heart, still describing his companion with disgust. The fire makes conversation possible without making either figure safe or reconciled. Perhaps that is what makes this moment recognizable. A creator may have to remain present to an unwelcome account before knowing what repair is possible, what demands should be refused, or how much blame belongs to whom. Agreeing to hear the story leaves those judgments open long enough for something more than revulsion to inform them.

A question to sit with

When something you helped create becomes a source of conflict, whose experience is hardest to hear without immediately defending your intentions?
