The Companion Who Has Already Been Spoken For

In the creature’s imagined escape from loneliness, a future companion’s wishes seem settled before she exists.

The creature offers Victor a remarkably detailed picture of a life that has not begun. Given a female companion, he will leave human society for the wilds of South America. They will eat acorns and berries, sleep on leaves, and live without harming anyone. His proposal gives loneliness a practical solution: two rejected beings, a distant home, enough food. But the companion whose presence makes this future possible has no opportunity to describe what she might want.

The request follows his account of murder and of placing incriminating evidence on a sleeping woman. His suffering is real within the story, and so is the suffering he deliberately causes. When he asks for companionship, his appeal carries both an intelligible need and a threat. Victor feels the force of that need, briefly considering what happiness he owes the being he made. The conversation is urgent, frightened, and crowded with consequences.

Within that argument, the creature treats his proposed companion’s agreement as something already secured. She will share his food, accept his exile, and attach herself to him because both will be excluded from the human world. The passage explicitly gives us these predictions. It does not give us her wishes. One way to read this absence is that his longing has become so consuming that he can imagine another person vividly as an answer, while scarcely imagining her as someone who might answer differently.

There is something persuasive about his expectation. Someone who has suffered similar rejection might understand him with less explanation. Shared circumstances can make companionship possible where it once seemed hopeless. Yet understanding and agreement are different things. Even if she experienced the world exactly as he expects, she might want a different home, different company, or solitude. His plan moves from the possibility of mutual recognition to the certainty of a shared life without pausing over that distance.

An ordinary version might arise between two friends who both feel out of place in their town. One finds an apartment elsewhere and begins planning the move for both of them. The rent is manageable together. Neither has enjoyed living where they are. They have spent evenings discussing how badly they want a change. From one friend’s perspective, the decision may feel nearly made. The other might still care about staying near a sibling, finishing a course, or leaving on a different timetable.

The difficulty is that the proposal can be generous and presumptuous at once. Finding the apartment takes effort. Imagining a better life together may express real affection. That effort can also make refusal harder: now someone has invested hope, research, perhaps money in an agreement that never quite happened. A person can find themselves being asked to approve a whole future when they thought they had only been sharing dissatisfaction.

The creature’s imagined arrangement is more extreme because he believes almost every other possibility has been closed to him. That helps explain the pressure he places on this one. It also makes the missing companion’s freedom uncomfortable to consider. If she could refuse him, his promised escape from misery would become uncertain again. The passage leaves room to wonder whether his confidence comes partly from being unable to bear another outcome. Sympathy for that desperation does not settle what she would owe him.

In a hypothetical friendship or partnership, leaving room for a separate answer could make a cherished plan fall apart. Two people might discover that their shared difficulty has led them toward different solutions. That would be a loss, especially for the person who had already pictured the kitchen table, the journey, the relief. Shelley’s scene makes that imagined future feel moving and troubling at once. Its comfort depends on a second person whose agreement the speaker has already supplied.

A question to sit with

When a shared future feels like your best hope, how much room have you left for the other person to imagine something else?
