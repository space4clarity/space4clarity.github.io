The Hurt Caused by Leaving a Decision to Someone Else

Elizabeth’s explanation of Bingley’s absence raises an uncomfortable possibility: a person can cause real hurt simply by failing to hold their ground.

Jane Bennet is trying to understand why Bingley has stopped coming to see her. Elizabeth believes his sisters and his friend have influenced him; Jane resists that explanation. If he truly cared for her, she reasons, they could not separate them. For Jane, accepting Elizabeth’s account would mean thinking badly of several people at once, including the man whose affection she had hoped for. She would rather believe she misunderstood his feelings.

Elizabeth introduces a less comfortable possibility. Someone need not deliberately arrange another person’s unhappiness to contribute to it. In her account, Bingley’s failure is a lack of resolution: he may have feelings of his own, yet fail to stand by them when other people press their wishes upon him. The passage gives us Elizabeth’s explanation, not direct access to Bingley’s motives. Still, the distinction she makes matters even before we know whether she has applied it correctly.

A failure to decide can look surprisingly innocent from the inside. A person waits because the conversation is awkward, because someone influential objects, or because tomorrow might bring greater certainty. Each postponement seems small. Meanwhile, someone else has to interpret the silence. Jane is left trying to work out whether the connection meant what she thought it meant. Whatever Bingley intends, his absence gives her a problem she cannot resolve by herself.

Imagine two friends planning to share an apartment. One has been enthusiastic, has toured places, and has discussed the budget. Then their family starts raising objections. They keep telling themselves they are still considering the arrangement, while their friend turns down another possible roommate. No one has plotted a betrayal. Yet the undecided person’s freedom to keep considering depends on the other person continuing to wait, with fewer options as the month passes.

The difficult point is that hesitation may have good reasons. Sharing an apartment is a substantial commitment. Family objections could reveal a real concern. People are allowed to reconsider, including after showing enthusiasm. But reconsidering creates something to communicate. The harm in the hypothetical situation comes partly from letting an earlier expression of interest remain in force while privately withdrawing from it. Uncertainty has consequences even when it has not become a final answer.

Jane’s argument puts considerable weight on the strength of affection: if Bingley cared enough, interference would fail. That is an understandable hope. It also makes affection carry the whole burden of action. Elizabeth’s alternative suggests that caring and being able to sustain a choice are separate capacities. Someone might feel warmly toward another person and still defer, avoid conflict, or let a more determined person settle the matter. Warmth alone offers the person waiting very little security.

There is a complication here for anyone trying to be fair. We cannot reliably measure another person’s affection from one delayed reply or abandoned plan. Elizabeth herself is making an inference, and Jane has reasons to resist it. Yet uncertainty about motives does not erase the practical situation. The prospective roommate still needs an answer. Jane still has to live with Bingley’s absence. It may be possible to leave someone’s inner character unsettled while being clear about what their conduct has made difficult.

Elizabeth’s observation draws attention to the moment when private indecision begins organizing another person’s life. That moment can arrive before anyone feels ready to make a firm choice. Recognizing it does not magically produce certainty or courage. It may only produce an awkward conversation in which someone admits that their earlier confidence has weakened. Such a conversation could disappoint the listener. It would also give them information that silence keeps withholding.

A question to sit with

When does taking more time to decide begin to spend someone else’s time without their agreement?
