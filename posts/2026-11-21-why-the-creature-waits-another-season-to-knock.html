Why the Creature Waits Another Season to Knock

The creature keeps preparing to meet the cottagers, and each gain in understanding gives him another reason to wait.

The creature in Mary Shelley’s Frankenstein has spent months watching a family from his hiding place beside their cottage. He has seen them care for one another and offer help to poor people who stop at their door. He wants to go to that same door and ask for friendship. By the time he tells Victor about the plan, he can explain both why he expected kindness and why he kept putting off the visit.

Some of the delay makes sense on its own terms. People who have seen him before have recoiled, and the cottagers have had time to become his whole idea of a welcoming household. A bad encounter would cost him the company he imagines having with them. He also gives a more curious reason for waiting. Because he can feel his understanding improving every day, he expects another few months to make him better equipped for the conversation. His progress makes postponement seem promising.

What he learns during the wait pulls him in several directions. Watching the family through autumn and into winter shows him how much their happiness depends on their affection for one another. It makes him want their company more. He also says that knowing more makes his own isolation clearer to him. I read those details as part of what makes readiness so elusive here. The more he can understand about the people he hopes to meet, the more he has to lose in the meeting he is preparing for.

Something smaller can happen when a person thinks about joining a local choir. Suppose they have sung mostly at home and decide to spend a few weeks learning to follow sheet music before they go to an open rehearsal. After a month they can follow the melody on the page. Now that they can read it, they start hearing where they slide into a note or take a breath halfway through a phrase. Another month of practice could genuinely help. It could also turn up three more things they would like to fix before anyone hears them.

From home, the singer can hear every missed entrance on a recording. At rehearsal, they could find out what happens when someone misses an entrance. Maybe the person beside them quietly points to the right bar. Maybe the director moves too fast for them to keep up. Either would tell them something about what it would be like to sing with this particular group. Practicing at home can change how they arrive, but the room and the people in it still have something to teach them.

The creature eventually comes up with a specific plan. He will speak to the blind De Lacey while the others are out, so that his voice can make an impression before his appearance does. He hopes the old man might then speak for him with the rest of the family. The passage ends with De Lacey alone after playing his guitar, with the conversation still ahead. By then the creature has learned enough to choose whom to approach and when. He still has to approach a man whose kindness he knows only from watching what it looks like when it is offered to someone else.

A question to sit with

What do you hope another month of preparation will tell you, and what could you learn only by showing up?
