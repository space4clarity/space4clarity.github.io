Jane Bennet Leaves Room for a Cooler Friendship

Jane Bennet imagines being on good terms with Bingley’s sisters again while allowing the friendship itself to change.

After Jane Bennet becomes engaged to Bingley, she learns something about the months they spent apart. He tells her that he had no idea she was in town the previous spring. Jane had found that hard to believe, and the news makes her happy. It also changes her understanding of his sisters. She concludes that they kept him in ignorance because they opposed the match. The passage gives us Jane’s inference here. What Bingley tells her is simply that he did not know she was there.

Jane immediately tries to imagine the sisters’ point of view. She thinks they wanted their brother to make a more advantageous match, and she hopes his happiness will bring them around. For a moment, it sounds as though this will end in a full restoration of the old friendship. Then Jane adds that they can never be to one another what they once were. She slips that judgment into the same sentence as her hope that they will get along.

Elizabeth hears a degree of unforgivingness unusual in her sister and is glad of it. She would hate to see Jane taken in again by Miss Bingley’s show of affection. That response gives Jane’s quiet qualification some weight. Jane never says how she intends to behave around the sisters. Still, her choice of words suggests that she can picture a pleasant visit with them without assuming she will again confide in them or count on their interest in her affairs.

Imagine two friends who have spent years taking long walks together and talking about their families. One learns that the other repeated a sensitive story at a dinner. They have a conversation about it, an apology is offered, and a few weeks later they end up walking home together from a mutual friend’s birthday. They fall easily into conversation about a shop that has closed and a dog they used to see on their route. For ten minutes, the friendship feels familiar enough that either of them might assume it has gone back to normal.

Then one asks how things are at home. It is a perfectly natural question, the sort the other friend once would have answered all the way down the block. This time she gives a short answer and asks about the dog. The walk can still be a pleasure for both of them. And the friend who asked may have to discover, in an ordinary moment like this one, that being welcomed back into conversation comes with fewer assumptions about what will be shared.

There is something awkward about a change that small. It may be hard to name without making a friendly evening feel like an extension of the original dispute. In Jane’s case, the engagement gives everyone a reason to be especially pleased when a visit goes well. The sisters could take her warmth as a sign that everything is as it was. We have Jane’s conversation with Elizabeth to tell us how much thought may sit behind the ease other people see.

Jane’s word for the future is stronger than the example of the walk requires. She thinks the old closeness can never return, and she says so at a moment when much else has just been happily put right. Austen lets that small certainty sit beside Jane’s expansive hopes for the marriage and for the family it will bring her into. Jane can look forward to seeing the sisters again and already know that some part of the visit will feel different to her.

A question to sit with

When a conversation feels easy again after a breach of trust, what might tell you how much closeness has actually returned?
