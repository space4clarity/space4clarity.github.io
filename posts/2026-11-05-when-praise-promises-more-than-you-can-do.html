When Praise Promises More Than You Can Do

Lydgate’s new reputation in Middlemarch reveals how admiration can burden someone with expectations their actual skill cannot support.

After Fred Vincy falls ill with fever, his family replaces Mr. Wrench with Lydgate as their medical attendant. The change quickly becomes town conversation. Some people condemn the Vincys; others celebrate Lydgate’s arrival as providential and credit him with exceptional skill in treating fevers. A sick young man’s care becomes a public contest over which doctor deserves blame and which deserves admiration.

Lydgate might be expected to welcome the favorable version. He has gained a patient and people are praising his ability. Yet the narrator explicitly describes this uninformed promotion as both humiliating and dangerous. The comparison with a weather prophet sharpens the problem: people may credit someone with predicting or controlling events whose uncertainty remains much greater than the reputation allows.

The passage does not establish precisely what either doctor could have prevented. It does show Mr. Vincy believing, confusedly, that greater concern for his important family might somehow have kept fever away. His complaint makes protection from illness sound like a service the household should have received. That expectation helps explain the danger in Lydgate’s praise. If the new doctor accepts credit for extraordinary powers, the family may expect those powers to keep working.

There is a recognizable modern dilemma here for anyone who becomes the person who can always fix something. Suppose a colleague recovers a damaged spreadsheet shortly before a deadline. The recovery required skill, but it also depended on a backup happening to exist. Soon the office treats that colleague as someone who can rescue any lost work. The compliment feels good. Correcting it requires explaining a limitation just when everyone is grateful.

That explanation can sound oddly ungracious. A relieved coworker wants to celebrate; the person being celebrated starts talking about missing files and the possibility of failure next time. It may seem easier to smile and let the exaggeration pass. But the next emergency arrives with an altered expectation. Someone may neglect a backup because they believe recovery is always possible, or promise a client a deadline on the assumption that their talented colleague will manage.

The difficulty is that some of the praise is deserved. Lydgate need not pretend to lack medical knowledge, and the hypothetical colleague need not deny a successful repair. The distinction lies between recognizing a contribution and assigning command over the outcome. A person can make a sound judgment, work carefully, and improve the chances without being able to guarantee what happens. Admiration often compresses those separate things into a reassuring story about the right person taking charge.

Eliot also keeps Lydgate from becoming a flawless guide to handling this. The narrator says his impatience with foolish expectations may lead him into damaging frankness. He can see the problem and still respond in a way that hurts his position. That detail matters because correcting inflated praise takes more than accuracy. People who are frightened or relieved may hear an honest limit as indifference, especially when they have placed their hope in someone's competence.

Mrs. Vincy’s suffering gives that hope its weight. She follows Lydgate and pleads for her son’s life. Her need is immediate and understandable; no careful account of professional limits could make it disappear. The passage leaves a hard space between what someone desperately needs and what another person can honestly promise. Perhaps the most difficult moment comes when praise invites a capable person to close that space with a certainty they do not have.

A question to sit with

When someone’s confidence in your ability exceeds what you can promise, what makes it difficult to explain the difference?
