Caleb Can Feel Sorry for Bulstrode and Still Leave

Caleb Garth’s refusal to keep working for Bulstrode explores the painful distance between feeling compassion and continuing a business relationship.

Caleb Garth has come to tell Bulstrode that a sick man needs a doctor. He has also come to end their business relationship. These purposes sit awkwardly together: Caleb is arranging help while withdrawing his services. He has carried the ill Raffles to shelter, heard something that convinces him Bulstrode has acted dishonestly, and decided he cannot continue working for the banker. He remains attentive to suffering throughout a conversation that causes more of it.

The passage does not give us Raffles’s full account, so we cannot assess its evidence independently here. What it does show is Caleb’s conviction and Bulstrode’s frightened effort to discover how much he knows. Caleb describes harmful conduct involving gain, deceit, and other people’s rights. He refuses the suggestion that a mutually profitable arrangement supplies sufficient reason to continue. The benefit itself has become part of what troubles him.

Yet Caleb makes no claim to understand the whole person standing before him. He allows that someone might repent while remaining entangled in the consequences of past actions. That possibility matters to him. The narrator explicitly describes his pity and his intention to spare Bulstrode unnecessary pain. His refusal is therefore especially uncomfortable: he can imagine reasons for sympathy without finding a reason to stay.

Bulstrode presses directly on that discomfort. Caleb says he does not want to make his life harder; Bulstrode replies that leaving does exactly that. There is force in the objection. Withdrawal can hurt even when it is quiet, private, and carefully explained. Caleb cannot make his decision painless by choosing a gentle tone. His answer acknowledges that he must leave anyway, without pretending the loss will feel merciful to the person losing him.

Consider a hypothetical freelance bookkeeper who discovers that a longtime client has deliberately withheld money owed to a small supplier. The client may be ashamed, under financial pressure, and frightened of losing another reliable person. Those circumstances could deserve serious attention. The bookkeeper might still decide that handling the client’s accounts has become intolerable. Understanding the client’s distress would not automatically settle whether to accept next month’s work.

What makes that decision difficult is often the wish to produce an explanation that nobody can reasonably resent. Caleb has no such explanation available. Bulstrode values his services and wants them to continue. Their interests have separated. Read this way, the scene suggests that a considerate refusal may remain a refusal someone experiences as abandonment. The hurt does not disappear merely because the person leaving has thought carefully about it.

There are limits to what Caleb’s conduct can settle for an ordinary modern dilemma. His promise of silence might feel generous in this exchange, but silence could be a different matter if others remained exposed to harm. Even his assurance leaves room for something unknown to require disclosure. The useful insight here is narrower than a complete guide to handling wrongdoing: pity need not determine whether a particular working relationship continues.

Caleb also gives up the comfort of declaring himself wholly superior. He knows enough, in his judgment, to stop working; he does not claim to know enough to pronounce on everything Bulstrode is or might become. That distinction leaves the conversation unresolved in a human sense. Bulstrode remains distressed, Caleb remains sorry, and the business connection ends. No shared feeling arrives to make the decision easier for both of them.

A question to sit with

When someone’s distress makes leaving feel cruel, what would help you decide whether continuing the relationship is something you can honestly offer?
