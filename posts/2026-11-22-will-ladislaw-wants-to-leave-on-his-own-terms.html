Will Ladislaw Wants to Leave on His Own Terms

Will Ladislaw is ready to leave the newspaper until Mr. Brooke gives him reason to think his departure would look like a dismissal.

Will Ladislaw is already thinking about leaving the Pioneer when Mr. Brooke comes to talk about its future. He is angry with Brooke and with himself, and he imagines that leaving Middlemarch could put him in a better position with Dorothea. In five years, political writing and speaking might earn him enough distinction that he could declare his love without feeling he was asking her to lower herself. He means to stay a little longer, though. Before he goes, he wants some sign that she cares for him and some way to let her know why he has held back.

Brooke has plans of his own. He has withdrawn from the election and spoken with men who might take over the newspaper. He tells Will how highly he rates his abilities, suggests that a more ordinary man could now run the paper, and offers to write letters of introduction. Will asks directly whether Brooke wants him to give up his post. Brooke answers by suggesting that Will himself might prefer a better opportunity. By the end of the conversation, even the question of whose idea it is to leave has become awkward.

Once Brooke is gone, Will decides that the family has been urging Brooke to get rid of him. The passage gives us Will’s conclusion, while Brooke has spoken chiefly about the paper and the people who might take it over. What’s striking is how quickly Will puts the question of leaving in different terms. A little earlier, he was weighing what he might accomplish elsewhere and what he hoped to learn from Dorothea before going. Now he is determined to make the timing his own. Staying has become a way of showing that he can leave whenever he chooses.

Suppose someone has been thinking about stepping down from a neighborhood committee. The meetings take up a weeknight, the work has grown stale, and there is already a rough plan to finish out the spring. Then the chair mentions that a newer member could take over and adds that everyone knows how busy the current volunteer has been. Suddenly, another term sounds bearable. There may even be a small pleasure in picturing the chair having to make room on the agenda for someone they had quietly expected to be gone.

That reaction can carry information worth having. Perhaps the volunteer has put years into the committee and wants the chair to ask what they want before discussing a successor. Perhaps there is unfinished work they still care about and the prospect of handing it over makes that clearer. There is also a harder possibility. The volunteer might now need the chair to welcome their staying before they can bear to leave. In that case, the chair’s approval has become a condition of carrying out a plan the volunteer was already making.

Will’s flash of pride makes sense in part because Brooke leaves him so little to answer. A direct request would give him something definite to accept, contest, or ask about. Instead, Brooke supplies an account of why Will might want to go and leaves Will to guess how much of it is courtesy. Yet Will’s private reply also shows how much power that guess has acquired. He can imagine leaving to build a career, or staying until he and Dorothea understand each other better. For the moment, what matters most is that whenever he goes, the people he suspects of wanting him gone should have no claim on the decision.

A question to sit with

If someone seemed ready for you to leave a role you were already thinking of giving up, what would you want settled before you could decide when to go?
