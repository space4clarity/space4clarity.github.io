Dorothea Is Moved by the Gallery Before She Looks at the Paintings

Dorothea’s visit to a gallery in Rome raises a question about what we actually enjoy when we stand before a celebrated work of art.

When Will Ladislaw calls on Dorothea in Rome, she has just been crying and dries her eyes before receiving him. Will notices the traces of tears. As she explains that her husband spends most of the day reading at the Vatican Library, Will has a hard time keeping his opinion of Casaubon to himself. He manages a smile, and when Dorothea asks what amuses him, he brings up a sketch of his that she once puzzled over. He remembers her remark as cutting. She remembers trying to make sense of a picture she found baffling.

Dorothea has been having a similar experience with paintings in Rome, although she describes a surprising moment before the bafflement. When she first enters a room covered with frescoes or hung with rare paintings, she feels awe. She compares herself to a child watching a grand ceremony. The robes and processions in her comparison suggest how much she takes in at once. Then she begins to look at each picture in turn. Some seem to lose their life as she studies them, while others retain a force she finds strange or even violent.

Dorothea puts the difficulty down to her own dullness. It is easy to see how she gets there. The room has made her feel that something worth seeing is within reach, and the reputation of the pictures gives her reason to expect that a closer look will bring it nearer. Instead, the feeling recedes. What interests me about the order of events is that she has already had a strong response by the time she starts wondering what is wrong with her. Her first response belongs to the room as a whole, and she seems to expect each painting to account for a share of it.

Will gives her a different way to think about what happens in a gallery. He says that some feeling for art comes with practice. Having tried to paint himself gives him some sense of how a picture comes into being. He also admits that part of the pleasure of knowing a style can be the pleasure of recognizing it. He enjoys the art in Rome immensely, yet he can imagine pulling that enjoyment apart and finding several sources for it. His answer leaves room for Dorothea to learn more without settling in advance which paintings she would enjoy if she did.

Imagine going to an exhibition with a friend who has been looking forward to it for weeks. In the first room, a row of enormous canvases makes you stop short. Your friend heads straight for the painting on the poster, and you follow. Up close, you read about the years the artist spent revising it, look for the changes described on the label, and drift back toward the doorway. From there, you can see the canvases together again. In a smaller painting near the exit, there is a hand resting on a table that you keep looking at, even after your friend has moved on.

On the way home, your friend asks what you thought of the exhibition. A quick verdict would leave out the pleasure you had from the doorway, the work you put into looking at the famous painting, or the few minutes you spent with that hand. Dorothea’s account is unusually precise about where her feeling changes. There is a little freedom in that precision. You might want another look at the famous painting after hearing what your friend saw in it, or you might go back just to see how the room feels when you first walk in.

A question to sit with

If you think of an exhibition that impressed you, where were you standing when you felt it most, and what could you see from there?
